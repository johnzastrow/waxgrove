Waxgrove — desktop layouts

Not the phone,
only wider

Mobile-first set the design order, but a stretched phone column wastes what desktop is actually for. These layouts start from a different question: which tasks are genuinely better with room and a keyboard?

Phone

Capture and consume. Add a song you just heard. Browse the grove on the bus. Play something. Rate it, tag it, leave a comment. Short, one-handed, interruptible.

Desktop

Curate and reconcile. Build a playlist from four sources at once. Work a queue of twelve ambiguous matches. Audit which tracks failed to export and why. Long, two-handed, focused.

Desktop 01

The Grove — density over scrolling

The phone shows title and artist. Desktop shows the whole canonical record — identifiers, provenance, and which services each record resolves to — because that is the information you need when deciding whether a match is right.

Desktop 01 · The Grove
Nine columns of canonical record versus the phone’s two. The rings still lead each row, so match quality is scannable down the left edge. Service pills show at a glance that “Everywhere” resolves on Spotify but not Apple — the kind of thing you only discover at export time on a phone.
What desktop buys

Density and comparison. You can see six records’ identifiers at once and spot that “Silver Springs” has no ISRC and no year — a weak record worth fixing before it reaches a playlist.

Desktop 02

The Crate — two panes, because adding and reviewing are one task

This is the layout with no phone equivalent. On a phone you search, add, navigate back, and check what you have. On desktop both halves are on screen at once: sources on the left, the crate on the right, and items move between them without ever losing your place.

What desktop buys

Simultaneity. The crate exists because playlists get built from many sources — and on desktop “many sources” stops being sequential. Drag-to-reorder and multi-select follow naturally; neither has a comfortable phone equivalent.

Desktop 03

Playlist — export status becomes a column

On the phone, partial export is a summary banner: “42 of 45 transferred.” On desktop it becomes per-track truth. You can see which three failed, and why, without tapping anything.

Queue left, comparison table right, keyboard through the lot. Every column exists to answer one question — is this the same recording? — and the duration delta usually settles it.
What desktop buys

Throughput. Twelve ambiguous matches is fifteen minutes of tapping on a phone and about ninety seconds with J K and a number key. This screen is the reason a self-hoster opens the laptop.

Desktop 05

How the tiers relate

One codebase, three tiers. Panes are progressively revealed rather than layouts being rewritten — every desktop pane exists on the phone as its own screen.

< 700 · phone

Single column. Panes become screens; the rail becomes a bottom bar. Review is one item at a time.

700–1100 · tablet

Rail plus one content pane. The crate splits horizontally rather than vertically; the playlist sidebar drops below the track list.

1100–1400 · laptop

Rail plus two panes. Full crate two-up and the review queue. The Grove’s detail aside is collapsible.

> 1400 · desktop

Rail plus two panes plus aside. All columns visible; nothing truncates.

The rule that keeps this honest — full parity, both directions

Every feature and function is available on every tier. Desktop is not a reduced view of the phone, and the phone is not a reduced view of desktop — only the ergonomics differ. The review queue is dramatically faster with a keyboard, but it must be completable on a phone one item at a time; equally, anything reachable on the phone must be reachable on desktop rather than being dropped as “mobile-only”. A tier that cannot finish a task is a bug, not a breakpoint.
